    How to read the unit circle

    The unit circle, also called the trigonometric circle, has radius 1 and its centre at the origin. Every angle θ, measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis, picks out exactly one point on it.

    The coordinates of that point are (cos θ, sin θ). Cosine tells you how far the point is to the right or left, sine how far it is up or down, and tan θ = sin θ / cos θ is the slope of the radius.

    Angles that differ by a full turn, 2π or 360°, land on the same point, which is why sine and cosine repeat. Negative angles go clockwise.

    Frequently asked questions

    What are sin and cos of 30°, 45° and 60°?

    sin 30° = 1/2 and cos 30° = √3/2; sin 45° = cos 45° = √2/2; sin 60° = √3/2 and cos 60° = 1/2. In radians these angles are π/6, π/4 and π/3.

    How do you convert degrees to radians?

    Multiply by π/180: for example 135° = 135 × π/180 = 3π/4. To go back to degrees, multiply by 180/π. This page accepts both: just add ° for degrees.

    Is there an easy way to remember the unit circle values?

    For 0°, 30°, 45°, 60° and 90° the sine is √0/2, √1/2, √2/2, √3/2 and √4/2, that is 0, 1/2, √2/2, √3/2 and 1. The cosine follows the same sequence backwards.

    Why is tan 90° undefined?

    Because tan θ = sin θ / cos θ and cos 90° = 0, so you would be dividing by zero. The same happens at every angle of the form π/2 + kπ, such as 90° and 270°.

    How do you find sin and cos of angles bigger than 90°?

    Use the symmetry of the circle. For example 150° is the mirror image of 30° across the y-axis, so sin 150° = sin 30° = 1/2 and cos 150° = −cos 30° = −√3/2. The sign depends on the quadrant the point lands in.
